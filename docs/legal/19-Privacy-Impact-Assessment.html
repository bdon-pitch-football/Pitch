1 · Executive summary

What was assessed, what was found, and what it costs us to fix.

Pitch collects and holds personal information about children, most of them between eight and seventeen, and makes some of it visible to adults who are not their parents. That is the point of the product — a pathway only works if somebody can see the player — and it is also the whole of the privacy risk. This assessment works through what we collect, who can see it, what could go wrong, and whether the safeguards already built are enough.

The finding is that the architecture is sound and the residual risks are concentrated in four places. The permission model, the guardian consent spine, the deliberate absence of any social layer for minors, and the refusal to collect geolocation, behavioural analytics or health information together put Pitch in a materially better position than a conventional platform retrofitting protections. What remains are risks that architecture alone does not solve.

What changed at v2.0, and why the risk picture moved

The product changed materially on 27 August. Six changes bear on this assessment, and five of them reduce risk — which is unusual for a scope expansion and is worth recording as the reason the conclusion below still holds.

  • The Interest Register was added and is paid — the first surface on which a child's disclosure reaches a third party. Assessed at R22.
  • The export was removed. A club can no longer download a list of children. This deletes the only object in the product that survived revocation, and it is the single largest risk reduction in this version.
  • Nothing reaches a club until a human has verified it — verification is manual, logged and revocable, and can never be set by payment. It also corrected a defect: "verified club" badges had been drawn before any verification existed.
  • Card payments arrived, and with them a new data class and a set of commercial mechanisms that could reach a child if built carelessly. Assessed at R23.
  • An edit to an under-16 page now returns to the guardian before it republishes. Closes a real hole and creates a new object — a pending version nobody has approved. Assessed at R24.
  • Share cards are guardian-approved, and a child's school is gone from every under-18 page. Both close gaps this assessment previously carried.

Version 1.1 note: the launch scope narrowed between v1.0 and v1.1 — the ground-status banner, the players-wanted notices and the sponsor route were cut for cost, and a rule was added barring any club from naming a person under 18 on a public page. Those changes were not made for safety reasons and they improved the safety position anyway. They are reflected throughout, most visibly at R6.

The conclusion of this assessment, stated once and stated plainly

As things stand today, the safeguards are not sufficient, and this assessment does not clear the product for launch. Version 1.0 said the safeguards were sufficient provided four things were built, and then said those four things would not be built in time. A Privacy Impact Assessment whose conclusion depends on a condition it expects to fail is useless at the only moment it exists for, which is the go/no-go on the morning of launch. So it is restated as a test that can actually be applied:

Four items are launch-blocking — recommendations 1, 2, 3 and 12 at §4.9. They have not changed since 25 August, and the 27 August scope expansion did not add to them. Each is small except the first. If all four are green on the day, this assessment supports launch. If any is red, this assessment does not, and proceeding is a decision taken against it rather than with it.

What that costs, honestly: the four total roughly three to four engineering days inside a window the register has already cut twice. That is a scope conversation for BUZ and Leo, not a privacy finding — but the privacy finding is that the alternative is opening a product to real children with a known, named, unmitigated hole in it. Recommendation 4, the safety operations layer, is deliberately no longer on this list — see §4.10 for why, and for what replaces it.

Overall conclusion

Not yet. This assessment does not support launch as things stand. It supports launch on the day recommendations 1, 2, 3 and 12 are green — a check that takes a minute to run and can be answered honestly at any go/no-go, on any date. A soft launch is a real launch: the gate does not move because the audience is small. Everything else in this document is sequenced work, and none of it is a reason to hold the date.

2 · Methodology

What we followed, and what we chose ourselves.

This assessment follows the ten steps in the OAIC's Guide to undertaking privacy impact assessments and is reported in the six-section format the Guide suggests. Analysis is presented against all thirteen Australian Privacy Principles even though Pitch is not currently bound by them (see §4.2), because the intention is to operate to that standard and to opt in when the timing is right.

Four further frameworks were adopted voluntarily as benchmarks, because the APPs alone do not address children well:

  • The Children's Online Privacy Code exposure draft of 31 March 2026 — its collection standard, consent line, best-interests test, PIA requirement and published PIA register are treated as the forward-looking standard even though the Code is not yet registered.
  • The UK ICO's Children's Code, fifteen standards, benchmarked at §4.7.
  • eSafety's Safety by Design principles, assessed at §4.8.
  • UNCRC General Comment No. 25, which supplies the best-interests reasoning at §4.5 and its requirement that the criteria applied be stated transparently.
A note on risk ratings

The OAIC prescribes no risk matrix, and this document does not claim its authority for one. Risks below are rated on a likelihood scale (rare · unlikely · possible · likely · almost certain) against a consequence scale expressed in the four harm categories the Children's Online Privacy Code exposure draft uses — physical, emotional, developmental and material — rather than in the financial terms an enterprise register would use. The consequence of a privacy failure here is a child, not a fine.

Two provisions this assessment relies on and could not verify

Stated here rather than in a footnote, because a good deal downstream rests on them and a reader is entitled to know which floorboards have not been tested.

Rule 4A of the Online Safety (Age-Restricted Social Media Platforms) Rules 2025, as amended in March 2026 — the additional condition requiring a recommender or a logged-in feature. Its operative text was obtained from the Federal Register of Legislation and is quoted accurately in doc 18. It has not been confirmed by a lawyer, and the conclusion that Pitch sits outside the under-16 regime rests on it more than on anything else.

Section 45 of the Child Protection (Working with Children) Act 2012 (NSW), headed "Unauthorised disclosure or dishonest collection of information". Four attempts across three hosts returned only subsection (3), which concerns the Children's Guardian's own disclosures. Subsections (1) and (2) and the penalty could not be obtained. The heading alone — "dishonest collection" — is squarely on point for a platform holding check numbers it is not the engaging organisation for. It is the first thing counsel should read.

Consultation. The launch screens were reviewed across five rounds, and the product design through three structured sessions in which the founder argued from the position of each affected person in turn — technical director, coach, parent, and player over eighteen. Warm-network families and coaches will run the full flow unaided during the shakedown weekend before launch, and their difficulties are treated as findings. Children themselves have not been consulted directly, and that is a gap — recommendation 11 addresses it.

3 · Project description

What the thing is. No analysis in this section, per the Guide.

Pitch is a web application at www.pitchfootball.com.au serving Australian football. At launch it does four things: it lets a player build and share a football CV; it lets a coach build a coaching CV; it lets a club publish a club page; and it publishes one chronological, filterable trials index. Account creation is restricted to Australia. Public CV and club pages are viewable worldwide.

A club page carries the club's philosophy, pathway, teams and age groups, its trial notices, an alumni list of former juniors and where they progressed to, and a sponsorship enquiry route. The trials index carries the trial notices of verified clubs alongside unclaimed listings compiled by Pitch from public club information and marked as unclaimed.

Two waitlists are already live or imminent: an Australian pre-launch page collecting an email address and a role from adults only, and a screen shown to non-Australian visitors collecting a country and, from adults only, an email address — under-18s are never asked for any detail, and only an anonymous count of country, age band and month is retained, with no IP address kept.

A club may subscribe to the Interest Register from launch (D-109, D-130): a player registers interest in a trial or in the club generally, and appears in a standing list the club works inside Pitch. The player's record does not travel with it. The club receives the player's name, age, current club, the squad, a preferred position, one line the player wrote, and a tokenised link to a page the family controls — not a file, not a copy, and no contact details. For a player under 16 the guardian presses send. There is no export at any tier (D-122). This is assessed at §3.3 and R19.

A person has one account. Roles are hats: a coach who is also a parent has one login. Date of birth drives an automatic age state machine with three bands — under 16, 16–17, and 18 and over — described in full at doc 09 and summarised in the flows below.

4.3 Children, capacity and consent

The OAIC's guidance is that the Privacy Act sets no age at which a person may make their own privacy decisions, that capacity turns on whether the individual has sufficient understanding and maturity to understand what is proposed, and that where case-by-case assessment is not practicable an entity may presume capacity at fifteen and its absence below. The Children's Online Privacy Code exposure draft would harden that into a bright line at fifteen.

Pitch draws the line at sixteen, and does not assess capacity case by case. Below sixteen, consent is given by a person with parental responsibility and the child's profile does not exist until that consent is recorded. Between sixteen and eighteen the player acts for themselves in relation to their own record, but is not discoverable by anybody until a guardian turns discoverability on (D-22 as amended, 3 September). The guardian retains visibility, may withdraw discoverability at any time, and contact continues to route to both.

Open — not yet decided

Whether a 16- or 17-year-old may accept the terms and privacy policy for themselves, or whether a guardian must co-accept until eighteen, is an open question before counsel (doc 18, question 7). A minor's contract is generally voidable, and our own inclination is guardian co-acceptance to eighteen. Nothing in this section should be read as settling it.

This is more conservative than both the current guidance and the draft Code, and deliberately so: the line is drawn where the product's own safety architecture already splits, which means a single mechanism enforces both. We record two consequences honestly. A fifteen-year-old who under the draft Code could consent for themselves cannot do so here. And a child's own wishes about their record are mediated through a guardian until sixteen — mitigated by the requirement that the child sees everything the guardian sees, and by the child's independent right to trigger deletion.

4.4 Automated processing

Nothing at launch. From the December release, two signals are generated automatically over assessment data: an attention flag where competency bands have not moved across blocks, and a progression flag where a player consistently reaches the top band. Both are surfaced only to club staff, are prompts a coach confirms or dismisses rather than verdicts, are measured against age-stage expectations rather than against squad mates, and are always shown with a relative-age context marker. Neither is visible to the player, and a parent sees their own child's progress and targets but never a squad comparison.

These are the automated decisions that would need to be described in a privacy policy under APP 1.7 and 1.8 from 10 December 2026 if Pitch is by then an APP entity. Doc 15 already describes them, ahead of the requirement.

4.5 Best interests of the child — the criteria applied

General Comment No. 25 asks not only that best interests be a primary consideration but that the criteria applied be stated transparently. Ours are five, and they are the tests every design decision was put to.

1 · Does it help the child be seen?

The product exists because talent goes unseen for reasons of postcode and connection. A privacy control that makes a child invisible to the people who hand out opportunities is not automatically in their interests. Visibility to verified clubs is a benefit, not merely a risk.

2 · Can an adult reach the child?

Being seen and being contactable are separable, and separating them is the central design move. Nothing that makes a child reachable by an adult outside their club is acceptable at any age below eighteen.

3 · Would the child mind, later?

A record follows a person for years. Anything that could embarrass an adult about their twelve-year-old self is excluded: no rankings, no numeric ratings, no language of deficiency anywhere in the product, and — when match tracking arrives — disciplinary events recorded but never shown on a minor's profile at all, never discoverable anywhere, visible only to club staff and the player's own family.

4 · Does the family keep control?

Consent revocable, link pausable, record deletable, everything the child sees visible to the guardian. Control that cannot be exercised in one action is not control.

5 · Does it serve the child, or us?

Applied hardest to engagement mechanics. Pitch never sends a message designed to bring someone back. No streaks, no "you haven't been on for a while", no notification to a minor that is not strictly functional, and no premium surface rendered on any under-18 account at all. A child's own development record is never behind a paywall, and a minor gets ten highlight clips free where an adult on the free tier gets three — because a cap a child cannot buy their way out of is charging them for being a child. Where a growth tactic and a child's interests conflict, the tactic loses. This is about what Pitch does, not about what a club does: a club ringing a family it has not seen for a month is a club doing its job, and R18 governs how that is presented to it.

Where the criteria pull against each other is the sixteen-to-seventeen band, where criterion 1 argues for discoverability by default and criteria 3 and 4 argue for opt-in. Resolved on 3 September in favour of opt-in, on the strength of the overseas pattern — the EU guidelines bind being found or contacted, the ICO code makes visibility high-privacy by default, and the closest comparator in this market gates recruiting discoverability on parental consent. The argument the other way is recorded as the cost of the decision rather than removed, because it was not wrong: a default-off setting would be left off by the least-connected families — the exact children the mission exists for — and that the harm being guarded against is contact, which criterion 2 already forecloses. We record that this is the assessment we are least confident in, and it is question 4 of the solicitor brief.

4.10 What a company of one person can honestly operate

Version 1.0 recommended a safety operations layer that included a 24-hour out-of-hours takedown capability, and it was challenged on the ground that a one-person company cannot staff it. That challenge is correct and the recommendation is rewritten. The right question is not what best practice describes; it is what we can keep every single time, including on the Sunday of a long weekend — because a published standard we miss once is worse than a smaller one we always meet, both in front of a regulator and in front of the family it was missed on.

Three things, separated because they are different problems:

What we publish

One business day to respond to any report, with an automated acknowledgment on receipt that says when a human will look. This matches the support commitment already made elsewhere in the project and does not invent a second, competing promise. It is deliberately unambitious and it is always kept.

What we build but do not publish

An emergency class — a child's identity exposed, an adult attempting contact, a report naming a child at risk — that bypasses the inbox and reaches the founder's phone directly. Target: hours while awake, first thing otherwise. Unpublished, because publishing it converts it into a promise; built, because it is what actually protects someone.

What removes the need for a roster

A one-action takedown that works from a phone. Any admin can make a page invisible in seconds without judging the merits, from anywhere, and reverse it later. A statutory removal window is met by capability, not by staffing — the answer to "what if a notice arrives at 9pm on a Friday" is that taking the page down takes fifteen seconds and asks no questions. This is the same build as recommendation 1's suppression switch, which is why recommendation 1 carries both. It must be tested from a phone before launch, not assumed.

Three further items from v1.0 are withdrawn or changed, and the reasoning was not ours.

  • Consulting under-16s directly during the shakedown weekend: withdrawn. Research with children carries its own consent and welfare requirements and does not belong in the same seventy-two hours as a launch. Recommendation 11 now consults guardians during the shakedown, and a proper child-consultation exercise is designed separately, with its own protocol, for the December sprint. This was a good recommendation put in an indefensible place.
  • Adjudicating a guardian dispute: never. Recommendation 1 asks for the technical capability to remove a guardian's access. It must not ask a founder to weigh two parents' accounts of a family. The process, which is triage and not judgment: act without question on a court order, a parenting order or a police request; short of that, the immediate no-questions action is suppression, not removal — the record goes dark for everyone while the family sorts out what it needs — and the family is pointed to 1800RESPECT. Removing a guardian's access permanently happens on an order, or not at all. The person answering that inbox must never be the person deciding who a child's parent is.
  • Five-year retention of report records: kept, but split. It appears to be required — the Basic Online Safety Expectations set a five-year record-keeping expectation for reports, subject to counsel confirming the provision — and the tension with our own minimisation posture is real. The resolution: retain the report event, the decision, the action taken and the timestamps for five years; minimise the identifying content inside that record; and where reported material itself is held as evidence, hold it separately, with restricted access, on a shorter clock unless a law-enforcement hold applies. What is required is proof that we handled a report properly. That is not the same as a five-year file about a child.

4.11 Age assurance — the answer to the question that was actually asked

R3 is the largest hole in this design, and it has been open since v1.0 without a proposed fix. The question put to this seat was: what does adequate age assurance look like for us, given that we refuse to collect government identity documents? Three parts, answered in order.

Is the club roster cross-check sound?

Yes, and it is the strongest measure available to us — but it must be built as a match, not as a collection. If a club sends us a roster, we have acquired a list of children from a third party, most of whom are not our users, which is over-collection on any reading. If instead we send the club a name and a date of birth we already hold and receive a yes or a no, we have verified what a guardian told us without acquiring anything. Build it as a one-bit answer. The honest limitation: it is worthless at launch, because no club is on the platform on the first day. The third rung of the waterfall does not exist for the first cohort and no document should imply otherwise.

Should a 16-or-over sign-up require guardian contact before activation?

For 16 and 17, yes — required, and not for age-assurance reasons. The locked architecture requires a 16–17 year old's guardian to turn discoverability on before anybody can find them, and routes all contact to guardian and player together. Neither is buildable if there is no guardian on the record. A 16–17 account without guardian contact is not weakly assured; it is a build that cannot honour two locked decisions. That makes it a defect rather than a question.

For 18 and over, no. Demanding a guardian's details from an adult is over-collection and would be refused by every adult who read it.

So what closes the 18-plus hole?

Nothing closes it completely without identity documents, and pretending otherwise would be the worst outcome available. What is left is a shift from prevention to detection, and two of the four are nearly free:

  • Lock the declared date of birth against upward amendment without guardian or club confirmation. Closes the declare-young-then-age-up path. Cheap. Recommendation 2.
  • The age contradiction is visible to us and we are not looking at it. An account claiming to be 19 that names a U15 squad as its current team is a contradiction the system can see for free, because it is football data we already hold. Hold the account at sign-up and route it to the inbox. This is probably the single highest-yield measure available and nobody has proposed it before now. Recommendation 2.
  • A report route for "this account belongs to a child", usable without an account and without certainty. Coaches and parents in a football community know who the children are; we do not. Recommendation 2.
  • Treat adulthood as a claim until something corroborates it — an unverified adult gets a reduced surface until a club affiliation or a second signal arrives. This is exactly how coach status is already treated. Design in December; too large for this runway.

Is refusing government ID right, or merely comfortable?

Right, and this seat would hold that line even if counsel said we could. The Privacy Commissioner's age-assurance guidance requires proportionality and data minimisation and is explicit that age assurance is not a blank cheque to use personal or sensitive information. Collecting an identity document from a child, or from a parent about a child, creates a breach consequence far worse than the one it prevents — and a document store is a target in a way that a football CV is not.

The honest other half: refusing identity documents means we cannot achieve high assurance, so we must never claim it. No Pitch document, screen or sales conversation may say or imply that we verify age. We ask; we confirm with a guardian on two separate channels; later we check with a club. That is what we do, and it is what the privacy policy says.

The thing to hold on to about R3

A child who lies about their age does not merely slip past the guardian gate. They place themselves in the adult band, where our protections deliberately stop — no guardian visibility, no routed contact, a public profile. The architecture protects children; it does not protect people it has been told are adults. That is why detection matters more here than a better front door, and why the age-contradiction hold is worth more than anything else on this list.

5 · Conclusion

Whether the safeguards are sufficient.

Pitch handles a great deal of information about children and makes some of it visible to adults, which is an unusual thing to build and an unusually consequential thing to get wrong. The assessment finds that the safeguards designed into the product — permissions computed rather than stored, a guardian gate that precedes the existence of a child's profile, an absolute prohibition on adult-to-child contact, the complete absence of a social layer for minors, and the refusal to collect location, behaviour or health data — address the great majority of the privacy risk before it arises, and do so structurally rather than by policy.

Four residual risks are not adequately treated today, and they are the substance of this document: a guardian who should not have access, with no way to remove one (R6); a child who declares an adult age and steps outside the entire architecture (R3); public pages about children that nothing currently stops a search engine indexing (R1); and third-party embeds that reach out from a page whose subject is a minor (R10). Recommendations 1, 2, 3 and 12 address them, and together they are roughly three to four engineering days.

Three further risks are treated but not closed, and each has a date rather than a fix: the safety operations standard, which must be published and operable on the day the reporting route goes live (R16, recommendation 4 and §4.10); the residual hand-copy risk in the Interest Register, now that the export is gone and only a contract term stands between a club and a screenshot (R19, recommendation 13); and the overseas waitlist (R20, recommendation 14).

The conclusion, restated as the test it needs to be: the safeguards designed into this product address the great majority of the privacy risk structurally, before it arises, and are better than what a platform of this size would ordinarily have. They are not sufficient as things stand. They become sufficient when recommendations 1, 2, 3 and 12 are green — and that is a check that can be run on the morning of any soft launch and answered yes or no, which is what a go/no-go needs. Everything else here is sequenced, and none of it is a reason to hold the date.
