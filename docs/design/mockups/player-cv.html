The public player CV — a better single column

This is the acquisition engine, the first thing a club ever sees, and the page the brief says must be the most beautiful thing we build. It measures 61% empty at 1280 — and the benchmark seat, the audit seat and I all reach the same answer independently: the void is at the sides of a well-made 604px column, and that is what a document is supposed to look like. Do not put a rail on it. LinkedIn's own logged-out profile — the page BUZ is actually picturing — is one column. And the rail LinkedIn does have on its members' view holds "People Also Viewed", which under a fourteen-year-old's CV is a recommender over children and a pillar-zero breach.

So this mockup makes the column better and the sides deliberate. Four moves, and none of them adds information outside the column, which means there is nothing to reflow at 1023px and nothing to lose in print.

What is wrong now

  1. No frame. Once the hero scrolls away there is no identity, no action, nothing to orient against — on a 1,660px page.
  2. The highlight façades are 604×96 rectangles with a play button in the middle of nothing. That is where most of the "empty" reading on this page comes from. D-97 forbids fetching a YouTube thumbnail before the viewer presses play — but it does not forbid the façade being ours.
  3. The sides are bare background, so the page reads as unfinished rather than framed.
  4. The squad-number watermark is clipped mid-glyph by the card's right edge and collides with the position map. Two decorative elements in one corner.
  5. The stat tiles arrive on a staggered entrance (0.28s + 0.09s each) and count up from zero — so every tile reads "0" for the first frames, on a CV built around never showing one. At 820 the three tiles form a descending staircase. On 4G at a ground, the staircase is what a club sees first.

What the change does for the club opening this link

The sticky identity strip means the technical director always knows whose page he is on and always has the one thing he can do with it, however far down he has read. It is 56px, --surface over --bg, one hairline, no shadow — and it prints away cleanly, which is the reason it is a strip and not a rail.

The façades fill their cards: our hero gradient, the pitch mark at low opacity, the clip title at 15px/800 and its line at 12.5px/500 sitting in the card rather than in a strip along the bottom, and the honest label — "YouTube · nothing loads until you press play" — which turns a privacy control into something the reader can see us doing. Same height class, no emptiness.

The sides become the page: the floodlight gradient continued and one very low-contrast pitch mark in each margin. No information goes there, ever.

And two fixes that cost minutes: the watermark is clipped to whole glyphs and moved off the map's corner, and the stat tiles arrive together at their real values rather than counting up from a zero the never-zero rule exists to abolish.

What it costs to build

Low. Move 1 is a small component that must also exist on the phone — it does, as the same strip. Move 2 is pure CSS on ClipCard. Move 3 is a background on the page wrapper. The animation fix is deleting an animationDelay and a counter.

What it does NOT change

Not one fact on the page, not one word, not the order of the sections. Not the 640px column. No rail, no "similar players", no view count, no follower count, no completeness score — none of which may ever appear on a page about a child. The never-zero rule is untouched and strengthened: Georgia's CV still renders three tiles and Nate's still renders two, and neither ever shows a nought.

The print work is not in this mockup. The benchmark's finding stands and is worth telling BUZ separately: LinkedIn could not make a two-column profile print and shipped a separate résumé renderer instead. Our print fast-follow should render a one-column document, not print the page.
